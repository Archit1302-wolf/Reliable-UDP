# Section 2.4: Channel Impairment and Adaptive Retransmission Timeout (RTO)

## 2.4 Channel and RTO

The network emulation layer mediates all communication between sender and receiver, subjecting raw packets to controlled physical-layer impairments through the `Channel.damage()` pipeline. For each packet arriving in either direction, impairment decisions are evaluated in strict sequential order: packet drop probability (returning an empty list if lost), bit-level payload corruption (inverting bits of a selected byte), packet duplication (producing two identical copies), transmission delay with bounded uniform jitter ($\text{delay} \pm \text{jitter}$), and intentional reordering delay. To guarantee deterministic repeatability across comparative protocol benchmarks, independent pseudorandom generators are allocated to each transmission direction ($\text{seed} \times 2$ for forward DATA and $\text{seed} \times 2 + 1$ for reverse ACK traffic). Consequently, repeated experiments with identical seeds encounter identical drop and corruption sequences (e.g., dropping exactly 12 packets in repeated 64 KB transfers under seed 4), while slight goodput variations arise solely from host thread scheduling. Standard packet transmissions strictly obey first-in, first-out (FIFO) ordering by clamping departure times to $\max(\text{arrival} + \text{delay}, \text{last\_departure})$; reordering is introduced by injecting an additional 30 ms hold-back delay on selected packets, allowing subsequent packets to overtake them legitimately on the wire.

Timer management relies on an adaptive estimator adhering to RFC 6298 (Jacobson/Karels algorithm). Starting from an initial one-second default, each round-trip time sample ($R$) updates the running variance ($\text{RTTVAR} \leftarrow 0.75 \times \text{RTTVAR} + 0.25 \times |\text{SRTT} - R|$) prior to the smoothed average ($\text{SRTT} \leftarrow 0.875 \times \text{SRTT} + 0.125 \times R$), establishing the baseline timeout as $\text{RTO} = \text{SRTT} + \max(G, 4 \times \text{RTTVAR})$ with a clock granularity floor $G = 10\text{ ms}$. Under sample inputs of 40 ms, 60 ms, and 50 ms, the estimator tracks real path latency smoothly (producing RTO estimates of 120.0 ms, 122.5 ms, and 110.9 ms). Upon timer expiry, exponential backoff doubles the timeout multiplier to mitigate congestion collapse, capped at 60 seconds. Following Kurose and Ross, any arriving ACK that successfully advances the window window resets the multiplier to unity via `forward()`, restoring the baseline estimate without starving pipelined flows. To eliminate ambiguity in RTT estimation, Karn's algorithm discards samples associated with retransmitted packets, preventing spurious timeout inflation.
